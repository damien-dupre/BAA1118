pandas and DataFrames

pandas is Python’s library for tables, imported as pd:

import pandas as pd
capitals = pd.DataFrame({"country": ["Ireland", "France", "Japan"],
                         "capital": ["Dublin", "Paris", "Tokyo"]})
capitals
country capital
0 Ireland Dublin
1 France Paris
2 Japan Tokyo
  • A DataFrame: named columns, and rows labelled by an index
  • Each key of the dictionary becomes a column, and its list the column’s values
  • One column on its own, capitals["capital"], is a Series

Five Operations

Almost every data question needs only five operations:

Operation What it does In pandas
Selection keeps columns df[["a", "b"]]
Reduction keeps rows df[df["a"] > 0]
Extension adds a column df["c"] = df["a"] * 2
Aggregation one row per group df.groupby("a")["b"].mean()
Combination joins two tables df.merge(other, on="a")

df is any table, and a, b and c are its columns. Each operation has its own section, in this order.

Filtering Rows

  • The square brackets around it keep the rows that are True
  • The numbers on the left are the index: each row keeps its label, here 744 to 746
  • Text goes in quotes, "Ireland"; numbers do not: gapminder["year"] == 2007

Comparisons

Operator Means
== exactly equal
!= not equal
> and >= greater than, greater than or equal
< and <= less than, less than or equal

The easiest mistake to make is one = instead of two:

gapminder[gapminder["country"] = "Ireland"]
SyntaxError: cannot assign to subscript here. Maybe you meant '==' instead of '='?

= stores a value, == compares two. Python stops, and suggests the fix.

Several Conditions, Several Values

recent = gapminder[(gapminder["year"] >= 2000) &
                   (gapminder["country"] == "Ireland")]
two = gapminder[gapminder["country"].isin(["Ireland", "France"])]
nineties = gapminder[gapminder["year"].between(1990, 1999)]
print(len(recent), len(two), len(nineties))
2 24 284
  • & for and, | for or, with each condition in its own round brackets. The words and and or do not work on columns
  • isin([...]) keeps any of several values; between(a, b) keeps a to b, both included
  • len() of a table counts its rows: 2, 24 and 284 here

groupby("continent") splits the rows into groups, as in the picture; ["lifeExp"].mean() applies the mean to each group and combines the results, with the continents as the index.

One Step per Line

Step by step, one name per step:

groups = g2007.groupby("continent")
means = groups["lifeExp"].mean()
means.sort_values(ascending=False)

The same steps as one chain:

(g2007
  .groupby("continent")["lifeExp"]
  .mean()
  .sort_values(ascending=False)
)

5. Joining Tables

Joining Two Tables

Two tables share a key: here, the chocolate bar’s name.

  • how="inner", the default, keeps the rows in both tables: rows 0 and 1
  • how="left" keeps every row of the left table, NaN where nothing matched

Source: UBC Master of Data Science, Programming in Python for Data Science

merge() in Practice

leads = pd.DataFrame({
    "continent": ["Africa", "Americas", "Asia", "Europe"],
    "lead": ["Ama", "Ben", "Chen", "Dana"],
})
joined = g2007.merge(leads, on="continent", how="left")
print(len(g2007), "rows before,", len(joined), "after;",
      joined["lead"].isna().sum(), "without a lead")
142 rows before, 142 after; 2 without a lead
  • on= names the key: a column in both tables, with matching values
  • Oceania has no lead: its 2 countries get NaN, a missing value, which .isna().sum() counts
  • With how="inner", those 2 rows would disappear without a warning: check the row count after every join
